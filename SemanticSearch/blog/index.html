Semantic Search, Without the Scary Math

"Eating less salt helps reduce hypertension" shares no words with "How can I lower my blood pressure?". Keyword search can't connect them. Semantic search scored them 0.647 out of 1. Here is where that number comes from, and when it beats keyword search on 1,271 real questions.

Meaning as a direction

Linguists noticed in the 1950s that words with similar meanings turn up in similar company. J. R. Firth's version: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps". Record that company as numbers and you get a list, and a list of numbers can be drawn as an arrow. (4, 3) means 4 steps right and 3 up.

Cosine similarity, one step at a time

With 384 numbers there is nothing to draw, so the angle has to come from the numbers alone. Cosine similarity does that in four steps and turns the angle into a score: 1 for the same direction, 0 for a right angle, −1 for opposite. Move b, or pick one of the arrows above, and watch each step:

  1. 1. Multiply
  2. 2. Add
  3. 3. Lengths
  4. 4. Divide

a stays at (4, 3). Step 1 multiplies matching positions, step 2 adds them (the dot product), step 3 measures each arrow's length with Pythagoras, step 4 divides.

Try e. It points exactly where a points, only twice as long, and the cosine is 1.00: same direction, whatever the length. Compared with b, e's dot product is 48, twice a's 24, but step 4 divides by e's length of 10 instead of 5, and the cosine comes back to 0.96, the same as a's. Without step 4, long arrows would score high against everything. The formula, for when you meet it elsewhere:

cosine = a · blength of a × length of b
a · b = a₁ × b₁ + a₂ × b₂ + … steps 1 and 2
length of a = √( a₁² + a₂² + … ) step 3
The same four steps with 384 numbers

For "How can I lower my blood pressure?" and "Eating less salt helps reduce hypertension.", the first four multiplications are −0.0321 × −0.0238, 0.1206 × 0.1193, −0.0035 × 0.0537 and 0.0289 × 0.0068. After 380 more, the total is 0.6474. Both arrows have length 1.0000 (section 04 explains why), so step 4 divides by 1 and the cosine is 0.647: the bar in section 01. No single product matters much. The score is hundreds of small agreements added up.

Words, sentences, paragraphs: one recipe

"Word embeddings", "sentence embeddings" and "document embeddings" sound like three different things. In a model like ours they are one recipe applied to different amounts of text, and they all come out as 384 numbers in the same space. Get this part right and the rest of semantic search makes sense. Three things people get wrong

A sentence's arrow is the average of its words' arrows.

The words are adjusted by each other first. Only then are they averaged. That adjusting is where word order, and which "bank" you mean, get in at all.

Averaging the words' own arrows, the river-bank and savings-bank sentences score 0.874, nearly the same. The model's real sentence arrows score 0.262. Plain word averages made all three sentence pairs look alike, between 0.82 and 0.88.

A longer text gives a richer, more detailed arrow.

It gives a more blended one. Every text ends up as 384 numbers, however long it is, and our setup reads only the first 128 pieces, about 100 words. The rest has no effect at all (section 09).

The question about parking permits scores 0.743 against the parking sentence alone and 0.507 against the paragraph that contains it. This is why search systems split long documents into chunks of a few sentences and embed each chunk.

Word, sentence and document embeddings are different kinds of thing.

Same model, same 384 numbers, same space. That is what lets a seven-word question be compared directly with a 200-word abstract, which is all semantic search does.

The question and every document in section 07 went through the same model. Arrows from two different models, though, can't be compared at all.

What makes the adjusting useful is training. The model's makers showed it about 1.17 billion pairs of texts that belong together: a Stack Exchange question and its accepted answer, a paper's title and its abstract, a Reddit comment and its reply. Training pulled each pair's arrows together and pushed unrelated texts apart. Its vital statistics:

384numbers per text
1.000length of every arrow
128word pieces read, at most, in our setup
6rounds of adjusting
8 msto embed a question, on a laptop CPU
"How can I lower my blood pressure?" → [ −0.0321, 0.1206, −0.0035, 0.0289, −0.0166, 0.0010, … 378 more ]

No single number means "blood" or "health". Only the direction of the whole arrow means anything. Documents are embedded once, when they are added to the search engine. At search time only the question is embedded, and the engine looks for the stored arrows pointing most nearly the same way. Because the two are embedded separately, this setup is called a bi-encoder. The Sentence-BERT paper puts the saving in numbers: finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences takes about 65 hours if a model reads every pair together, and about 5 seconds if each sentence is embedded once.

The arrows measure "about the same thing", which is close to "means the same thing" but not equal to it. "Hot" and "cold" appear in the same kinds of sentences, so they sit fairly close. A researcher testing negation (NevIR, 2024) found most retrieval models do no better than chance at telling a document from its negated version. And to the model, order 4471 is just "an order number", while the person searching wants that one order.

Names, codes, gene symbols and rare terms are keyword search's strength and meaning search's weakness (a 2021 study found the same for questions about less common people and places). That is why most production systems run both and merge the two lists. Merging is part 3.

How much of each document the model reads

We found this one by accident. The model card says it reads up to 256 word pieces. LangChain4j, the Java library we run it with, ships a tokenizer file that stops at 128, so everything after roughly the first 100 words is ignored. We checked on the longest FiQA document: its arrow and the arrow of its first 126 pieces have a cosine of 1.0000. Identical. Then we re-embedded every document with the limit raised:

Two results. First, with 256 pieces all three datasets match the scores the MTEB leaderboard publishes for this model: 0.317, 0.645 and 0.369, against 0.316, 0.645 and 0.369. The model is fine; the 128 cut was the whole gap. Second, reading more is not automatically better. SciFact's abstracts gained +0.030 from being read in full. NFCorpus and FiQA did slightly worse read in full than at 256 pieces, and NFCorpus ended below the 128 cut. A whole long document becomes one average of several parts, the blending from section 04, and the model was trained on at most 128 pieces. We didn't measure which of the two matters more.

The practical rule: find out how many pieces your library actually reads, don't trust the model card, and test the limit on your own data. For long documents, splitting them into chunks, each with its own arrow, avoids both the cut and the blending.

Other libraries can do the same. Spring AI's TransformersEmbeddingModel (2.0.1) loads a tokenizer file with the same 128 cut and doesn't scale its arrows to length 1. We read that in its source code; we didn't run it.

Finding the nearest arrows fast

Comparing the question with every document is exact search. On FiQA that is 57,638 comparisons of 384 numbers each, for every question. Vector databases skip most of them with an HNSW index (Hierarchical Navigable Small World): each document is linked to a few of its nearest neighbours, in layers from sparse to complete. FiQA has 16 times as many documents as NFCorpus and exact search took 9 times as long, while HNSW stayed near 1 ms. For a few thousand documents, exact search is fine. For millions, it isn't.

Cosine, dot product or distance?

Vector databases make you choose how to compare arrows. OpenSearch offers cosine (cosinesimil), dot product (innerproduct) and straight-line distance (l2). Switch between them for the five sentences from section 01:

  1. Ways to bring down high blood pressure without medication.
  2. Eating less salt helps reduce hypertension.
  3. Blood pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury.
  4. Lower the tyre pressure before driving on sand.
  5. The stock market fell sharply today.

Highest cosine first.

The order never changes. Because every arrow has length 1, the dot product is the cosine (step 4 divides by 1), and distance falls exactly as cosine rises: distance² = 2 − 2 × cosine (0.542² = 0.294 = 2 − 2 × 0.853). Across all 1,271 questions and every document, the three gave the same top 10 1,271 times out of 1,271. So with length-1 arrows, use dot product: it is the cheapest. With other arrows, use cosine, because dot product favours long arrows. And when reading OpenSearch scores, note that cosinesimil reports (1 + cosine) ÷ 2: a cosine of 0.853 shows as 0.927.

In code

With LangChain4j the model runs inside the Java process, with no server and no API key:

EmbeddingModel model = new AllMiniLmL6V2EmbeddingModel();
float[] vector = model.embed("How can I lower my blood pressure?").content().vector();  // 384 numbers

In OpenSearch, the arrows sit in a knn_vector field next to the text, so one index serves both kinds of search:

PUT /nfcorpus
{
  "settings": { "index": { "knn": true } },
  "mappings": {
    "properties": {
      "title":     { "type": "text" },
      "text":      { "type": "text" },
      "embedding": {
        "type": "knn_vector", "dimension": 384,
        "method": { "name": "hnsw", "engine": "lucene", "space_type": "cosinesimil" }
      }
    }
  }
}

POST /nfcorpus/_search
{ "size": 10, "query": { "knn": { "embedding": { "vector": [-0.0321, 0.1206, …], "k": 10 } } } }

Exact search uses a script_score query with "source": "knn_score". The lab's whole client is SearchIndex.java.

Summary

  1. An embedding is an arrow of 384 numbers.Cosine similarity compares directions: multiply, add, divide by the lengths. (02, 03)
  2. Words, sentences and paragraphs go through one recipe.Split into pieces, adjust each by its neighbours, average. Longer text means a more blended arrow, not a richer one. (04)
  3. It finds answers in other words, and blurs names, numbers and "not".(01, 05)
  4. It wins when the answers share few of the question's words.FiQA +0.127, SciFact −0.032. 229 : 85 when few words are shared. (07, 08)
  5. Check how much text your model reads.Ours read 128 pieces, not the 256 its card promises. (09)
  6. HNSW is nearly free.Same top 10 as exact search for 94% of questions, in about 1 ms. (10)
