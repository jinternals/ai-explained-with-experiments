Meaning as a direction

Linguists noticed in the 1950s that words with similar meanings turn up in similar company. J. R. Firth's version: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps". Record that company as numbers and you get a list, and a list of numbers can be drawn as an arrow. (4, 3) means 4 steps right and 3 up.

Cosine similarity, one step at a time

With 384 numbers there is nothing to draw, so the angle has to come from the numbers alone. Cosine similarity does that in four steps and turns the angle into a score: 1 for the same direction, 0 for a right angle, −1 for opposite. Move b, or pick one of the arrows above, and watch each step:

  1. 1. Multiply
  2. 2. Add
  3. 3. Lengths
  4. 4. Divide

a stays at (4, 3). Step 1 multiplies matching positions, step 2 adds them (the dot product), step 3 measures each arrow's length with Pythagoras, step 4 divides.

Try e. It points exactly where a points, only twice as long, and the cosine is {{e:longer_arrow.cosine_with_a|2}}: same direction, whatever the length. Compared with b, e's dot product is {{e:longer_arrow.dot_with_b|0}}, twice a's {{e:longer_arrow.dot_a_with_b|0}}, but step 4 divides by e's length of {{e:longer_arrow.length|0}} instead of 5, and the cosine comes back to {{e:longer_arrow.cosine_with_b|2}}, the same as a's. Without step 4, long arrows would score high against everything. The formula, for when you meet it elsewhere:

cosine = a · blength of a × length of b
a · b = a₁ × b₁ + a₂ × b₂ + … steps 1 and 2
length of a = √( a₁² + a₂² + … ) step 3
The same four steps with 384 numbers

For "{{e:question|text}}" and "{{e:recipe_384.sentence|text}}", the first four multiplications are {{e:recipe_384.first_products.0.question|4}} × {{e:recipe_384.first_products.0.sentence|4}}, {{e:recipe_384.first_products.1.question|4}} × {{e:recipe_384.first_products.1.sentence|4}}, {{e:recipe_384.first_products.2.question|4}} × {{e:recipe_384.first_products.2.sentence|4}} and {{e:recipe_384.first_products.3.question|4}} × {{e:recipe_384.first_products.3.sentence|4}}. After 380 more, the total is {{e:recipe_384.dot|4}}. Both arrows have length {{e:recipe_384.length_question|4}} (section 04 explains why), so step 4 divides by 1 and the cosine is {{e:recipe_384.cosine|3}}: the bar in section 01. No single product matters much. The score is hundreds of small agreements added up.

Words, sentences, paragraphs: one recipe

People talk about "word embeddings", "sentence embeddings" and "document embeddings" as if they were three different things. In a model like ours they are the same thing: whatever text goes in, one arrow of 384 numbers comes out, made by the same four steps.

384 directions can't be drawn, so here is a toy version with just two, and we give them names: money to the right, nature and water up. Watch what happens to "bank".

    A toy with two named directions, to show the idea. The real model's 384 directions have no names, and it keeps every word piece, including "the" and "of"; the toy shows only the three words that matter.

    One way to picture step 3: every word is at a meeting. It arrives with its own opinion, listens to everyone else in the room, and shifts its view to fit the conversation. "Bank" at a meeting with "river" and "water" ends up talking about nature. The text's arrow is then simply the room's average view.

    That also explains the other two sizes. A single word is a meeting of one: nobody to listen to, so "bank" stays in the middle, halfway between money and nature. A paragraph is a bigger meeting: the average of more voices, so if it covers several topics, its arrow sits between them and points clearly at none.

    Three things people get wrong

    A sentence's arrow is the average of its words' arrows.

    The words are adjusted by each other first. Only then are they averaged. That adjusting is where word order, and which "bank" you mean, get in at all.

    Averaging the words' own arrows, the river-bank and savings-bank sentences score {{e:levels.sentences.0.cosine_of_word_averages|3}}, nearly the same. The model's real sentence arrows score {{e:levels.sentences.0.cosine|3}}. Plain word averages made all three sentence pairs look alike, between {{d:word_avg_min|2}} and {{d:word_avg_max|2}}.

    A longer text gives a richer, more detailed arrow.

    It gives a more blended one. Every text ends up as 384 numbers, however long it is. And in our setup the model only ever sees the first 128 word pieces, roughly 100 words, because that is where the Java library cuts the text (see "Meet the model" below). The rest has no effect at all.

    The question about parking permits scores {{e:levels.paragraph.sentences.2.cosine|3}} against the parking sentence alone and {{e:levels.paragraph.whole|3}} against the paragraph that contains it. This is why search systems split long documents into chunks of a few sentences and embed each chunk.

    Word, sentence and document embeddings are different kinds of thing.

    Same model, same 384 numbers, same space. That is what lets a seven-word question be compared directly with a 200-word abstract, which is all semantic search does.

    The question and every document in section 07 went through the same model. Arrows from two different models, though, can't be compared at all.

    Meet the model

    Everything on this page uses one small, free model, all-MiniLM-L6-v2, from the Sentence Transformers project. Its name spells out what it is: a MiniLM (Microsoft's small, compressed version of Google's BERT) with 6 Layers, which the Sentence Transformers team then trained to make embeddings; v2 is their second version. What makes its adjusting useful is that training: its makers showed it about 1.17 billion pairs of texts that belong together, such as a Stack Exchange question and its accepted answer, a paper's title and its abstract, a Reddit comment and its reply. Each pair's arrows were pulled together and unrelated texts pushed apart.

    Output
    384 numbers per text, scaled to length 1
    Vocabulary
    30,522 word pieces. Text is lowercased first, so "Bank" and "bank" are the same piece
    Adjusting rounds
    6 layers (the "L6"), each letting every piece look at every other
    Size
    about 23 million weights, a 90 MB file that runs on a laptop CPU: {{b:datasets.nfcorpus.timing_ms.embed_question|0}} ms per question here
    Combining pieces
    the average of all pieces' arrows (mean pooling), step 4 above
    Training
    about 1.17 billion pairs of related texts

    No single number means "blood" or "health". Only the direction of the whole arrow means anything. Documents are embedded once, when they are added to the search engine. At search time only the question is embedded, and the engine looks for the stored arrows pointing most nearly the same way. Because the two are embedded separately, this setup is called a bi-encoder. The Sentence-BERT paper puts the saving in numbers: finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences takes about 65 hours if a model reads every pair together, and about 5 seconds if each sentence is embedded once.

    Different orders blur

    The arrows measure "about the same thing", which is close to "means the same thing" but not equal to it. "Hot" and "cold" appear in the same kinds of sentences, so they sit fairly close. A researcher testing negation (NevIR, 2024) found most retrieval models do no better than chance at telling a document from its negated version. And to the model, order 4471 is just "an order number", while the person searching wants that one order.

    How much of each document the model reads

    A model on its own is just a file of numbers. Something has to cut the text into word pieces, feed them in, and read the 384 numbers out. In this lab that is LangChain4j, a Java library for working with AI models. It ships with its own copy of all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and of the settings file that decides where text is cut.

    1. What should happen.The model can take up to 512 word pieces, and its makers chose to cut at 256 (the table in section 04).
    2. What happens in LangChain4j.Its settings file cuts every text at 128 pieces, roughly the first 100 words, before the model sees it. LangChain4j also has code to handle long texts by splitting them into parts and averaging them. Because of the 128 cut, that code never runs.
    3. How we know.FiQA's longest document is {{l:datasets.fiqa.longest_document_probe.tokens|int}} pieces long. We embedded the whole document, then only its first 126 pieces (plus the two markers). The cosine between the two arrows was {{l:datasets.fiqa.longest_document_probe.shipped_vs_its_first_126_tokens|4}}: the same arrow. Nothing after the first 128 pieces had any effect.
    4. How much it matters.Most documents are longer than that: {{l:datasets.nfcorpus.share_longer_than.128|pct}} of NFCorpus papers, {{l:datasets.scifact.share_longer_than.128|pct}} of SciFact abstracts and {{l:datasets.fiqa.share_longer_than.128|pct}} of FiQA answers.
    5. What we tried.We changed the setting and embedded every document twice more: once cut at 256, the makers' choice, and once with no cut, so LangChain4j splits long texts and averages the parts.

    We found this by accident, while checking why our SciFact score was lower than the published one. Here is what each setting gives:

    Two results. First, with 256 pieces all three datasets match the scores the MTEB leaderboard publishes for this model: {{l:datasets.nfcorpus.ndcg@10.256|3}}, {{l:datasets.scifact.ndcg@10.256|3}} and {{l:datasets.fiqa.ndcg@10.256|3}}, against 0.316, 0.645 and 0.369. The model is fine; the 128 cut was the whole gap. Second, reading more is not automatically better. SciFact's abstracts gained {{d:length_gain.scifact.none|signed3}} from being read in full. NFCorpus and FiQA did slightly worse read in full than at 256 pieces, and NFCorpus ended below the 128 cut. A whole long document becomes one average of several parts, the blending from section 04, and the model was trained on at most 128 pieces. We didn't measure which of the two matters more.

    The practical rule: find out how many pieces your library actually reads, don't trust the model card, and test the limit on your own data. For long documents, splitting them into chunks, each with its own arrow, avoids both the cut and the blending.

    Other libraries can do the same. Spring AI's TransformersEmbeddingModel (2.0.1) loads a tokenizer file with the same 128 cut and doesn't scale its arrows to length 1. We read that in its source code; we didn't run it.

    Cosine, dot product or distance?

    Vector databases make you choose how to compare arrows. OpenSearch offers cosine (cosinesimil), dot product (innerproduct) and straight-line distance (l2). Switch between them for the five sentences from section 01:

    1. {{e:sentences.1.text|text}}
    2. {{e:sentences.0.text|text}}
    3. {{e:sentences.2.text|text}}
    4. {{e:sentences.3.text|text}}
    5. {{e:sentences.4.text|text}}

    Highest cosine first.

    The order never changes. Because every arrow has length 1, the dot product is the cosine (step 4 divides by 1), and distance falls exactly as cosine rises: distance² = 2 − 2 × cosine ({{e:sentences.1.euclidean|3}}² = {{d:dist_squared|3}} = 2 − 2 × {{e:sentences.1.cosine|3}}). Across all {{d:total.queries|int}} questions and every document, the three gave the same top 10 {{d:total.metrics_same_top10|int}} times out of {{d:total.queries|int}}. So with length-1 arrows, use dot product: it is the cheapest. With other arrows, use cosine, because dot product favours long arrows. And when reading OpenSearch scores, note that cosinesimil reports (1 + cosine) ÷ 2: a cosine of {{e:sentences.1.cosine|3}} shows as {{d:opensearch_score|3}}.

    In code

    With LangChain4j the model runs inside the Java process, with no server and no API key:

    EmbeddingModel model = new AllMiniLmL6V2EmbeddingModel();
float[] vector = model.embed("How can I lower my blood pressure?").content().vector();  // 384 numbers

    In OpenSearch, the arrows sit in a knn_vector field next to the text, so one index serves both kinds of search:

    PUT /nfcorpus
{
  "settings": { "index": { "knn": true } },
  "mappings": {
    "properties": {
      "title":     { "type": "text" },
      "text":      { "type": "text" },
      "embedding": {
        "type": "knn_vector", "dimension": 384,
        "method": { "name": "hnsw", "engine": "lucene", "space_type": "cosinesimil" }
      }
    }
  }
}

POST /nfcorpus/_search
{ "size": 10, "query": { "knn": { "embedding": { "vector": [-0.0321, 0.1206, …], "k": 10 } } } }

    Exact search uses a script_score query with "source": "knn_score". The lab's whole client is SearchIndex.java.

    Summary

    1. An embedding is an arrow of 384 numbers.Cosine similarity compares directions: multiply, add, divide by the lengths. (02, 03)
    2. Words, sentences and paragraphs go through one recipe.Split into pieces, adjust each by its neighbours, average. Longer text means a more blended arrow, not a richer one. (04)
    3. It finds answers in other words, and blurs names, numbers and "not".(01, 05)
    4. It wins when the answers share few of the question's words.FiQA {{d:gain.fiqa|signed3}}, SciFact {{d:gain.scifact|signed3}}. {{d:groups.low.better|int}} : {{d:groups.low.worse|int}} when few words are shared. (07, 08)
    5. Check how much text your model reads.The model can take 512 pieces and its makers set 256, but our Java library gave it 128. At 256 our scores match the published ones. (04, 09)
    6. HNSW is nearly free.Same top 10 as exact search for {{d:total.hnsw_same_share|pct}} of questions, in about 1 ms. (10)
